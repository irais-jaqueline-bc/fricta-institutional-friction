# 4. Results

## 4.1 Overview of Adoption Friction

The final analytical dataset consisted of 81 childcare institutions. The theoretical FRICTA Adoption Friction Score (AFS_theoretical) exhibited substantial variability across institutions (mean = 0.416, SD = 0.151), suggesting that digital adoption conditions differ considerably across the sampled organizations.

This variation provided an opportunity to examine which institutional characteristics most consistently distinguish organizations experiencing high levels of digital adoption friction from those experiencing lower levels.

## 4.2 Friction Emerges Across Multiple Institutional Dimensions

All four FRICTA branches were positively associated with adoption friction. Operational limitations (OLI) showed the strongest branch-level association with AFS_theoretical (r = 0.847), followed by organizational constraints (OCI, r = 0.735), infrastructure constraints (ICI, r = 0.681), and human-capacity and readiness indicators (HCARI, r = 0.603).

Importantly, no single branch completely explained adoption friction. Instead, friction appeared to emerge from the interaction of operational, organizational, infrastructural, and human-capacity conditions. This pattern supports the multidimensional structure proposed by the FRICTA framework.

However, the relative strength of the branches suggested that some institutional dimensions may play a more influential role than others in distinguishing high-friction environments.

## 4.3 Institutional Factors Associated with Adoption Friction

At the individual-factor level, infrastructure and digital maturity indicators consistently occupied the highest-ranking positions.

The strongest individual association with adoption friction was observed for device availability (device_constraint, r = 0.694). Other highly ranked factors included record-keeping system limitations (r = 0.652), digital usage constraints (r = 0.643), resource limitations (r = 0.620), and lack of digital tool diversity (r = 0.608).

Notably, several traditionally cited barriers—such as staffing limitations, training deficits, and time constraints—did not appear among the strongest correlates.

This ranking pattern suggests that adoption friction may be linked more strongly to institutional digital maturity than to isolated human-capacity challenges.

## 4.4 High-Friction Institutions Exhibit a Distinct Digital Maturity Profile

To examine whether friction corresponded to recognizable institutional profiles, institutions were separated into low-friction and high-friction groups.

The largest differences were not observed in staffing, training, or workload variables. Instead, the strongest contrasts involved previous implementation experience, resource constraints, device availability, record-keeping practices, and digital usage patterns.

Institutions with high adoption friction consistently demonstrated lower levels of digital integration and weaker digital operational infrastructure. Conversely, lower-friction institutions tended to exhibit stronger evidence of previous technological adoption, more mature digital workflows, and greater technological availability.

These findings suggest that adoption friction is associated with broader institutional digital maturity rather than with any single isolated barrier.

## 4.5 Testing Competing Explanations of Adoption Friction

A central objective of this study was to evaluate two competing explanations for digital adoption friction.

The first explanation proposes that friction primarily emerges from deficiencies in institutional digital maturity, including limited technological infrastructure, weak operational integration, and insufficient implementation experience.

The second explanation proposes that friction is driven primarily by human-capacity limitations, such as staffing shortages, training deficits, and time constraints.

To compare these explanations directly, a hypothesis stress test was conducted.

Digital maturity deficit produced a standardized separation effect of 1.535 between low-friction and high-friction institutions. In contrast, human-capacity deficit produced a substantially smaller effect of 0.899.

The magnitude and consistency of this difference suggest that institutional digital maturity distinguishes high-friction institutions more effectively than isolated human-capacity barriers.

This result represents the central empirical finding of the study.

## 4.6 Robustness of the Main Finding

Multiple robustness analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the digital maturity finding depended on specific analytical assumptions.

First, threshold robustness testing demonstrated that digital maturity remained the stronger discriminator under 20/80, 25/75, and 30/70 percentile definitions of friction groups.

Second, sensitivity analysis showed that institutional rankings remained stable under alternative weighting schemes. Top-10 overlap remained between 80% and 90% across all tested weighting configurations.

Third, branch-removal analysis demonstrated that the digital maturity explanation remained dominant in three of four leave-one-branch-out conditions. The only exception occurred when the infrastructure branch (ICI) was removed, indicating that infrastructure plays a particularly important role in the overall friction structure.

Taken together, these results suggest that the central finding is not an artifact of a single threshold definition, weighting strategy, or branch configuration.

## 4.7 Infrastructure as a Critical Friction Component

Because infrastructure appeared repeatedly throughout the analysis, an additional decomposition was performed on ICI-related components.

Among all infrastructure-related indicators, device availability emerged as the strongest discriminator between low-friction and high-friction institutions (standardized difference = 1.595).

This result was stronger than internet stability, digital tool variety, and even the aggregate human-capacity deficit measure.

The finding suggests that access to basic technological infrastructure may function as a foundational prerequisite for broader digital adoption within childcare institutions.

## 4.8 Main Empirical Finding

Across institutional ranking analyses, profile comparisons, hypothesis stress testing, threshold robustness analyses, branch-removal experiments, and infrastructure decomposition procedures, a consistent pattern emerged.

Digital adoption friction was more strongly associated with indicators of institutional digital maturity than with isolated human-capacity barriers.

High-friction institutions were characterized not merely by shortages of time, personnel, or training, but by a broader pattern of limited digital integration, weaker implementation histories, reduced technological availability, and less mature operational practices.

Among all evaluated indicators, device availability emerged as the strongest individual institutional characteristic associated with adoption friction.

Although these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, they consistently support the interpretation that institutional digital maturity represents a central dimension of adoption friction within the analyzed sample.
